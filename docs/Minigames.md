# ARIA Minigames

## Purpose

ARIA minigames should make the learner use Gaeilge to accomplish something rather than test whether the learner can recognize a translation.

Core design rule:

> You do not win ARIA minigames by knowing about Gaeilge. You win by understanding enough Gaeilge to accomplish the task.

A useful filter for every proposed minigame: if Gaeilge could be replaced with arbitrary symbols and the game would still work essentially the same way, the mechanic is not doing enough language teaching.

## Shared Interaction Loop

Most ARIA minigames should reuse a common interaction pattern:

**Need → Communicate → Listen → Interpret → Act → Consequence**

This allows different scenarios to share underlying mechanics while changing setting, vocabulary, complexity, and goals.

## Proposed Minigames

### Out and About

Functional conversation and everyday needs in an explorable Irish town.

Examples:
- Find the restroom.
- Say you are hungry or thirsty.
- Ask where the Garda station is.
- Say you are lost.
- Ask for help.
- Ask someone to repeat or slow down.
- Shop and understand prices.
- Follow directions.

This is the first minigame to design in detail because it establishes the reusable interaction engine for other ARIA activities.

See `Out-and-About.md`.

### The Pub Order

The learner receives, places, or fulfills increasingly complex orders in Gaeilge.

Progression can move from nouns and numbers to quantities, adjectives, modifiers, questions, and full conversational exchanges.

### Market Day

The learner buys items using Gaeilge for objects, numbers, quantities, colors, descriptions, prices, and conversational exchanges.

The mechanic should require understanding enough language to obtain the intended item and pay correctly.

### Lost in Galway

The learner follows spoken or written directions through a small street map or explorable environment.

Useful language includes:
- Left and right
- Locations
- Prepositions
- Landmarks
- Asking for clarification

### The Hearth

A spatial-comprehension game centered on prepositions and object placement.

The learner manipulates a scene according to Gaeilge instructions, such as putting an object on, under, beside, behind, or near another object.

This should remain distinct from functional survival conversation.

### Who's Who?

Several characters appear. Maeve or another NPC describes one in Gaeilge and the learner identifies the correct person.

Potential language:
- Appearance
- Clothing
- Family relationships
- Adjectives
- Position and location

### Story Reconstruction

The learner hears or reads a short story and restores illustrated events to the correct sequence.

Higher levels can introduce cards that differ primarily by tense, person, or subtle meaning.

### Conversation Gauntlet

The learner navigates a short NPC interaction where multiple responses may be grammatically possible but only some fit the conversational context.

The goal is pragmatic communication rather than selecting a single artificial "correct answer."

### The Session

A pub/music-session environment where musicians, people, instruments, furniture, and other objects become interactive language targets.

Tasks may combine vocabulary, descriptions, directions, and prepositions—for example, identifying a person or giving an object to someone described in Gaeilge.

## Design Direction

Minigames should feel like different uses of one ARIA world rather than disconnected quiz modules.

Whenever possible:
- Reuse locations and characters.
- Reuse previously learned phrases in new contexts.
- Let the environment provide feedback.
- Allow more than one communicatively appropriate response when realistic.
- Let language proficiency unlock capability rather than merely unlock numbered lessons.
- Reduce conventional help UI as the learner gains Gaeilge phrases for requesting help inside the world.
